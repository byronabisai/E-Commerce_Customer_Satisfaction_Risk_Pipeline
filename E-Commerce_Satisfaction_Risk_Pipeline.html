Predicting At-Risk Customer Satisfaction in E-Commerce Orders¶

End-to-End ML Pipeline¶

1. Problem Statement¶

Business problem. The company records a customer_rating (1–5) after each order but this signal arrives after the customer has already had a poor experience. If dissatisfaction could be anticipated at order-processing time, the Customer Experience (CX) team could intervene proactively (priority shipping, a courtesy call, a service recovery voucher) before the customer forms a negative impression reducing churn, negative reviews and refund/return costs.

Business value. Even a modest lift in identifying at risk orders lets CX teams target interventions at the ~50% of orders most likely to disappoint, instead of guessing or contacting everyone (costly) or reactive only. This directly supports retention and customer lifetime value (CLV).

Data collection. In production, this pipeline would pull from the order-management system at checkout/fulfilment time (product, price, discount, region, payment method, promised delivery window) i.e. fields available before the customer experiences the product, not paid channels that already carry rating information. Richer signal could later be added from CRM (support-ticket history), logistics (actual vs. promised delivery time) and review text sentiment.

ML formulation. I'll frame this as binary classification: predict at_risk = 1 if customer_rating ≤ 3 (dissatisfied/neutral) else 0, using only pre-outcome order attributes (category, region, price, discount, quantity, delivery days, payment method, order timing). This is deliberately not a regression on revenue, since revenue is an exact deterministic function of quantity × unit_price × (1-discount) in this data (correlation = 1.00) predicting it would be a data leakage exercise with no genuine modelling value.

Evaluation metric choice. Because classes are balanced accuracy is meaningful as a headline number but the business cost of missing a dissatisfied customer (a lost, silent churner) is higher than the cost of a false alarm (an unnecessary but harmless courtesy voucher). I therefore report Precision, Recall, F1 and ROC-AUC, with Recall and ROC-AUC prioritized for model selection and use ROC-AUC as the primary cross-validation scoring metric since it is threshold independent.

df['order_date'] = pd.to_datetime(df['order_date'])

4. Model Training & Hyperparameter Tuning¶

Four candidates are compared with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on ROC-AUC: a majority class dummy baseline, Logistic Regression (interpretable linear baseline), Random Forest and Gradient Boosting (non-linear ensembles capable of capturing interactions).

In [10]:
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=RANDOM_STATE)

models = {
    'DummyMostFrequent': DummyClassifier(strategy='most_frequent'),
    'LogisticRegression': LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, random_state=RANDOM_STATE),
    'RandomForest': RandomForestClassifier(random_state=RANDOM_STATE),
    'GradientBoosting': GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=RANDOM_STATE)
}

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
cv_results = {}
for name, model in models.items():
    pipe = Pipeline([('prep', preprocessor), ('clf', model)])
    scores = cross_val_score(pipe, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring='roc_auc')
    cv_results[name] = scores
    print(f"{name:20s}  mean ROC-AUC = {scores.mean():.3f}  (+/- {scores.std():.3f})")

6. Final Discussion¶

Strengths of the pipeline. The pipeline follows sound machine-learning practice from end to end: thorough exploratory analysis, a target and evaluation metric chosen to fit the business problem, strict separation between training and test data to avoid leakage, several candidate models benchmarked against a naive baseline, a systematic search over hyperparameters, evaluation on a genuinely held out set and transparent feature-importance reporting. This same structure would transfer cleanly to a richer or larger dataset without needing to be redesigned.

Limitations. The main limitation lies in the data itself not the modelling approach: customer_rating shows almost no correlation (|r| < 0.02) with any of the available fields and every model tested linear or ensemble, tuned or untuned tops out at an AUC of roughly 0.52, only marginally better than the 0.50 random baseline. This points to satisfaction being driven largely by factors absent from this table product quality or defect rates, actual versus promised delivery performance, support-interaction history, packaging condition or review sentiment for example.

Business implications. Deploying this particular model would give the CX team only a marginal edge over simply contacting a random 50% of customers nowhere near enough of an improvement to justify basing targeting decisions or an intervention budget on it.

Recommendations.

  1. Do not deploy the current model into production decision-making; it offers negligible real-world lift.
  2. Enrich the data: join in delivery SLA-adherence, product return/defect rates, support-ticket counts, and free-text review sentiment (via NLP) before re-attempting this task.
  3. Consider reframing the problem around a variable with a demonstrable data-driven signal, e.g. the revenue prediction (where quantity/price already gave a near-perfect relationship) for demand or pricing use cases, or investigate causes of low ratings qualitatively (e.g. survey/text analysis) first.
  4. If a revised dataset does yield a usable AUC (e.g. > 0.70), redo this pipeline and pilot the model as an advisory ranking (not an automated action) with A/B-tested human in the loop review.

Explainability. Random Forest importances give a transparent, auditable ranking of which fields the model leans on (unit_price, discount_amount, unit_price_per_qty_ratio) satisfying basic model governance needs even though predictive power is low; SHAP values would add per order local explanations if this model were ever piloted for individual decisions.

Would we deploy? it cannot be deployed in its current form. Shipping a near-random classifier as a "satisfaction risk score" would erode CX-team trust in data science outputs and misallocate the intervention budget. The right next step is a data-collection initiative not further tuning of this model a conclusion this rigorous evaluation exists specifically to surface.
